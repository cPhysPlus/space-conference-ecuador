About the conference

Why eSPACE matters

A new generation of observatories, exascale computers, and AI-driven pipelines is reshaping how we understand the universe — yet access to that frontier remains profoundly unequal.

eSPACE was founded to close that gap. By bringing world-leading researchers to Ecuador's Yachay Tech University, we create a setting where cutting-edge science meets a regional community eager for mentorship, collaboration, and access. The conference is deliberately designed not just as a meeting, but as a catalyst: for new research partnerships, for early-career scientists entering the field, and for cross-disciplinary ideas that do not fit neatly into any single conference track.

A distinctive focus of eSPACE is the transfer of methods between astrophysics and adjacent fields. Techniques developed to simulate stellar winds, model plasma turbulence, or process radio telescope data have direct applications in space weather modelling, materials in space, and naval research. eSPACE creates the conditions for those conversations to happen.

In its fourth edition, the conference builds on three years of growing momentum, an expanding network of regional institutions, and a commitment to making high-level science genuinely accessible.

ONRG Partnership

The involvement of the Office of Naval Research Global reflects a deliberate commitment to bridging astrophysics and ONR-relevant science — including hydrodynamic simulation, adaptive sensing, and materials in extreme environments.

Associate Professor, Universidad Andrés Bello
Astrophysics Institute, UNAB, Chile

Maria Celeste Artale is an Associate Professor at Universidad Andrés Bello (UNAB) in Chile. Her research spans theoretical astrophysics, with emphasis on the host galaxies of binary compact object mergers, short gamma-ray bursts and X-ray binaries, high-redshift galaxies as protocluster tracers, and the connection between dark matter haloes and galaxy properties.

Eleanor E. B. Campbell

Professor Emeritus of Chemistry, University of Edinburgh • Fellow of the Royal Society

Eleanor E. B. Campbell is Professor Emeritus of Chemistry at the University of Edinburgh. A Fellow of the Royal Society, she held a chair at the University of Gothenburg and led a department at the Max Born Institute, building her career on the physics of highly excited molecules, nanoclusters and carbon nanostructures. Her research bridges femtosecond laser spectroscopy, ion–surface interactions and the behaviour of molecular materials under extreme excitation.

Juan Diego Soler

Astrophysicist, University of Vienna

Juan Diego Soler is an astrophysicist at the University of Vienna's Astronomy Department. He develops statistical tools to study the interstellar medium (ISM), combining radio and far-infrared observations with numerical simulations to understand the physical processes that shape the clouds of gas and dust in the Milky Way. Beyond research, he writes science outreach columns for El Espectador and is the author of two books on Antarctic astronomical explorations.

Astrophysics Data analysis Science communication

Enrique Vázquez-Semadeni

Senior Researcher, IRyA-UNAM
Instituto de Radioastronomía y Astrofísica, Morelia, Mexico

Enrique Vázquez-Semadeni is a senior researcher at the Instituto de Radioastronomía y Astrofísica (IRyA) of UNAM. He obtained his PhD in Astronomy from the University of Texas at Austin and served as Director of IRyA from 2015 to 2019. His research focuses on the interstellar medium, compressible magnetohydrodynamic turbulence, and star formation, with landmark contributions to understanding the density probability distribution in isothermal turbulence and the global hierarchical collapse model of molecular clouds. He is the author of Interstellar Flow and Star Formation (AAS–IOP Astronomy, 2024) and recipient of the Premio Universidad Nacional 2021 in the exact sciences.

eSPACE is intentionally interdisciplinary. We welcome contributions across all of the following areas and their intersections.

Climate & sun in January

Ibarra sits at ≈ 2,200 m in the Andean highlands and has a mild, spring-like climate year-round. January falls in the dry season: mostly clear days with the occasional shower, especially in the afternoon.

Daytime highs ≈ 16–20 °C; nights cool down to ≈ 7–10 °C. Pack a jacket!

UV radiation is intense at this altitude and latitude: forecasts for Ibarra frequently rate UV as very high, even on days that feel mild or partly cloudy. Sunscreen (SPF 30–50+), UV400 sunglasses, and a hat are essential, and drink plenty of water while you acclimatise.

What to pack

  • Sunscreen SPF 30–50
  • UV400 sunglasses & hat
  • Jacket or sweater
  • Walking shoes
  • US-style plug adapter, if needed
  • Travel insurance documents

⚠ Visas & entry requirements

Most participants — including citizens of the United States, Canada, the European Union, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Japan and most Latin American countries — enter Ecuador without a visa for tourism for up to 90 days.

Since 1 September 2025, nationals of 45 jurisdictions (mostly in Africa and Asia) need an advance visa, including for transit through Ecuador. The full list of countries can be found here.

Need a visa invitation letter? The organising committee can issue one. Please email e.space2027@yachaytech.edu.ec.

Also, your passport must be valid for at least six months from the date you enter Ecuador, with at least one blank page for entry/exit stamps. Passports that don't meet this may be refused entry. Immigration officers may also ask for proof of onward/return travel; this is rarely checked in practice, but it's worth having ready.

Health & vaccinations

No vaccinations are required to enter Ecuador. Since August 2025, Ecuador no longer requires the yellow fever certificate from people coming from Amazonian countries. However, the yellow fever vaccine is still recommended.

We also recommend you visit a travel-medicine practitioner before departure and keep routine immunisations up to date.

The venue sits at ≈ 2,200 m: most visitors feel no ill effects, but rest and hydrate during your first day, and go easy on alcohol until you acclimatise.

Community standards

Code of conduct

eSPACE is committed to being a welcoming, inclusive, and professionally respectful event for everyone. All participants — speakers, attendees, organisers, and staff — are expected to uphold these principles throughout all conference activities.

Respect and inclusion

We welcome participants of all backgrounds, career stages, nationalities, genders, identities, and abilities. Discriminatory behaviour of any kind will not be tolerated.

Professional conduct

All scientific exchange should be conducted with intellectual honesty and collegial respect. Critique ideas, not people. Harassment of any form is grounds for immediate removal from the event.

Safe environment for early-career researchers

We are especially attentive to creating conditions in which students and early-career scientists feel safe asking questions, presenting their work, and networking.

Photography and recording

Do not photograph or record other participants, their slides, or their conversations without explicit permission. Organisers will indicate which sessions may be recorded.

Social media

When sharing content from eSPACE publicly, respect speakers' wishes. If a speaker asks that their talk not be shared, honour that request immediately.

Reporting

If you experience or witness a violation, please contact any member of the organising committee in confidence. Retaliation against anyone who raises a concern is itself a violation of this code.

All participants are expected to adhere to the AAS Code of Ethics, which covers conduct toward others, harassment, sexual harassment, and bullying in detail. Organisers reserve the right to remove any participant who violates this code, without refund.

Destination

Ecuador & Imbabura

Ecuador is one of the most ecologically and culturally diverse countries on earth — four worlds in one: the Pacific coast, the Andean highlands, the Amazon basin, and the Galápagos archipelago. Despite its small size, it holds more bird species than the entire North American continent.

Imbabura, known as the Province of Lakes, sits in the northern highlands at around 2,200–2,500 m above sea level, and is home to crater lakes, ancient indigenous markets, and the imposing Imbabura volcano.

Ibarra, the provincial capital, is the conference venue city. Known as the White City for its colonial architecture, it sits at 2,200 m in a broad Andean valley and offers a lively cultural scene, excellent restaurants, and easy access to the rest of the province.

Otavalo hosts one of the largest and most famous indigenous markets in the Americas, held every Saturday — a short bus ride from Ibarra. Nearby, the Cotacachi & Cuicocha Lagoon offers a stunning crater lake set inside an extinct volcano, with trails and boat tours around the islands.

Urcuquí, about 20 minutes from Ibarra, is home to Yachay Tech University — the City of Knowledge — set against the Imbabura volcano on a purpose-built research campus. January is dry season: clear skies, warm days, and cool evenings.

Quito, Ecuador's capital and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is two hours from Ibarra by road — a natural stopover with its colonial old town and Baroque churches.

The Galápagos Islands — the living laboratory that inspired Darwin's theory of evolution — lie 1,000 km off the Pacific coast, reachable by a short domestic flight from Quito.
